    Key figures¶

    Four numbers that summarise how far the instrument can be trusted: what each measures, how it was obtained, and what it does not show.

    Figure In one line
    0.020 samples How closely the measured code offset (the signal's delay) matches a delay that was known in advance
    0.014 Hz How closely the measured Doppler matches a known Doppler, on the same test
    4 detectors How often each detector reports a defect that is not there
    Exactly 0 What comparing elements adds when the elements are identical

    The first two come from synthetic signals whose truth is known because the test built them. The last comes from the in-house GNSS simulator. None of them takes the device under test's own word for anything (Validating the instrument explains why that matters).

    0.020 samples: code-offset accuracy¶

    What it measures. The code offset is where in the recording each satellite's spreading code starts, which is the signal's delay. It is the number a receiver turns into range, so an error in it is an error in position.

    How it was obtained. A synthetic GPS L1 C/A recording is built with a code offset, Doppler and signal strength set in advance:

    Setting Value
    Signal GPS L1 C/A, one satellite
    Sample rate 12 MHz
    Length 200 ms, the length of a typical RF-simulator capture
    Signal strength 48 dB-Hz, typical of an outdoor satellite
    Noise 12 independent noise draws (seeds)

    The acquisition engine points at a coarse, 4-bit copy of the recording, as it would on real data. The instrument then measures the original samples, and the result is compared with the offset that was injected.

    The result, over the 12 seeds:

    Samples Time Range
    RMS error 0.020 1.7 ns 0.50 m
    Mean error (bias) +0.015 1.3 ns 0.37 m
    Worst seed 0.042 3.5 ns 1.05 m
    Acceptance gate 0.05 4.2 ns 1.25 m

    At 12 MHz one sample is 83.3 ns, or about 25 m of range.

    How to read it. The scatter matches the thermal floor: the error that noise alone imposes at this strength and length, about 0.015 samples over 200 ms. What remains is noise, not the instrument's code. A stronger or longer signal gives a smaller error, and a weaker or shorter one a larger error.

    Why it once read 0.009, and why 0.020 is the honest figure. The earlier test signal snapped its chip edges to the sample grid by the same rule the instrument used, so the two errors cancelled and the test measured nothing. The test signal and the instrument now place chips by deliberately different methods. They differ from each other by about 0.009 samples, and that is the floor the test sits on. Against a band-limited signal, which is what a real recording holds, the instrument's replica is exact. The full story is in The instrument's own errors.

    What it does not show. Accuracy on a real generator's signal, or at other sample rates. The other 16 supported signal components are held to the same test on four noise draws each, rather than twelve (Supported signals).

    0.014 Hz: Doppler accuracy¶

    What it measures. Doppler is the frequency shift from the satellite's motion relative to the receiver. An error in it is an error in the measured velocity, and it accumulates into carrier phase.

    How it was obtained. On the same 200 ms test signal, injected with a Doppler of 1234 Hz, the measured Doppler is within 0.014 Hz of the injected value. At L1 that is about 2.7 mm/s of line-of-sight velocity. The acceptance gate is 1 Hz.

    How to read it. Acquisition only finds Doppler to within tens of hertz, the spacing of its search grid. The instrument then measures it directly, about three orders of magnitude finer. That gap is the reason the tool has its own correlator.

    What it does not show. Doppler is assumed constant across the analysis window. That is right for hundreds of milliseconds and wrong for minutes: on long captures it leaves a small code bias (Status and roadmap).

    4 detectors, with measured false-alarm rates¶

    What it measures. The detectors turn a series of measurements into findings:

    Detector Looks for
    quantize Delay snapped to a grid, which shows as a staircase
    ripple A smooth periodic error, such as an interpolator leaves
    step A sudden jump
    stability Drift, scatter and outliers

    A detector is only as useful as its false-alarm rate, meaning how often it reports a defect in a series that has none.

    How it was obtained. Each rate was measured over thousands of trials on series known to be clean, and of several kinds, not just white noise:

    Detector Measured false-alarm rate Target
    quantize 0.6–1.4 %, over 6000 trials on three kinds of grid-free series 1 %
    ripple 1.10 % on white noise, 0.20 % on a random walk, 0.40 % on a walk plus noise 1 %
    step at or below its configured rate configured

    A known defect also survives the whole instrument: grids of 1.0, 0.5 and 0.25 samples, injected into a test signal, come back from the end of the chain to within 1 %.

    How to read it. A rate measured this way can be trusted at the stated value. Three of the four real defects found while building the detectors passed a single-case test and failed a rate.

    What it does not show. How small a defect can be found. That depends on signal strength more than capture length, and is about a quarter sample at 65 dB-Hz on 300 ms (Reading results honestly).

    Exactly 0: the inter-element chain adds nothing¶

    What it measures. With several elements (RF outputs), the tool compares each against a reference element. To do that it matches measurements by time and satellite, undoes the wrap at each code period and removes the carrier-phase starting point. Any one of those steps can add an error that looks exactly like a real difference between elements.

    How it was obtained. The in-house simulator generated a 4-element capture with all elements at the same position (zero baseline), so every element received the same signal. Every difference came back exactly zero: 8 satellites, 3 differences against the reference element, 400 epochs each.

    How to read it. Anything non-zero would have been the tool. A mismatched satellite, a one-epoch slip or an unhandled wrap each make the result non-zero, so exactly zero rules all of them out at once.

    What it does not show. That a non-zero difference is measured correctly. That is checked separately: on a synthetic 3-element capture with delays of +4.0 and −2.5 samples built in, the tool recovers both within four standard errors of the noise.
