    Code jumps at every power-of-two second¶

    A generator's code timing jumped late in short bursts, at elapsed times of exactly 2^k seconds. One rounding decision at an exact chip boundary caused it.

    +0.22 sampleslate at each event: about 18 ns, or 5.4 m of pseudorange
    2^k secondsonsets at 1/32, 1/16 … 1, 2 s of elapsed time; each event about twice as long as the last
    32 of 32events late, never early
    7 → 0events in the same capture after the fix, with the noise floor unchanged

    The symptom¶

    On one strong satellite with the others suppressed, the code-offset residual showed short excursions of about +0.2 samples, each with a small loss of prompt amplitude. On a first 200 ms capture there were three, and they looked aperiodic. Longer captures showed the pattern:

    • Onsets fall at 2^k seconds of elapsed time, after one constant offset per satellite.
    • Spacing doubles and duration doubles: 2, 3, 4, 7, 13, 25 epochs.
    • Magnitude does not change: about 0.22 samples across six octaves.
    • Always late.

    With ten satellites at equal power the events were invisible: the mutual-interference floor was 0.12 samples. Suppressing the other nine by 50 dB brought the baseline down to 0.0024 samples, and the events stood out at z = 47 to 92.

    What the instrument could say about it¶

    It is in the transmitted signal, not added at the output. Over twelve captures (four satellites at three times of day), the time of the first event moved with each satellite's propagation delay at a slope of +1.0001. A defect at a fixed point in the transmitted signal appears in the recording displaced by the delay, which is slope 1. A defect added after the delay would give slope 0.

    It is a real delay, not a distorted correlation peak. At each jump the early tap fell and the late tap rose. A flattened or attenuated peak would pull all three taps down together. This peak kept its shape and slid late, and the amplitude loss matched what a pure delay predicts to within 5 %.

    It is not one signal's. Galileo E1B at the same carrier and sample rate showed the same jumps at the instants predicted from the GPS measurements, with nothing refitted. So the cause was in a path both signals shared.

    It is not noise, power or Doppler. Two runs of the scenario were byte-identical. The measurements were identical at −80 and −120 dBm. Event size did not track Doppler over a 23× range.

    The cause¶

    The generator computed each sample's chip index as the floor of a floating-point chip phase. At the common sample rates the true chip phase at every chip edge is an exact integer, so floor was deciding an exact tie on a rounded value. When the rounding landed one unit in the last place low, the chip index came back one too small, and the previous chip was held one sample too long. That is always late, because it is floor.

    It was never a precision shortage. Whether rounding recovers the integer depends on a race between two power-of-two rounding steps, so it fails in windows just after elapsed time crosses each power of two. That produces the onsets, the doubling, and the constant magnitude. A 64-bit mantissa missed as many edges as a 53-bit one. The fix was to keep the boundary decision out of floating point altogether, carrying the chip phase as an exact integer and remainder.

    Sample rate / chip rate Peak code error Epochs hit in 2 s
    12.276 / 1.023 MHz (exactly 12 per chip) 0.246 samples, 6.0 m 63 of 2000
    24.552 / 10.23 MHz (L5) 0.019 samples, 0.23 m 1290 of 2000
    12.5 / 1.023 MHz 0.001 samples, 0.02 m 13 of 2000

    No sample rate is clean, and the common ones are the worst: every rate that is a whole multiple of 1.023 MHz puts every chip edge on a tie.

    Verified by re-measuring¶

    The same 2 s capture, the same detector, the same delays, against the fixed generator:

    Before After
    events above 0.05 samples 7 0
    shift at the predicted instants +0.21 to +0.31 samples ±0.001
    sign 7 of 7 late random
    baseline sigma 0.00034 samples 0.00034 samples

    The predicted instants became quieter than a control set of instants where the pattern predicts nothing, and the noise floor did not move: the events were gone, not smoothed away. The same tie also ran through the generator's subcarrier signs for BOC signals. Removing it cut Galileo E1B's apparent measurement noise by 13 to 150×.

    What it shows about the tool¶

    • Transients of 2–31 ms and 5 m are invisible to a receiver's tracking loops and to a position fix. An open-loop series at 1 ms resolution shows them directly.
    • The tool's measurements located the defect, inside the generator and in the transmitted signal, before anyone read the generator's code.
    • One instrument bug was found on the way: acquisition results for signals with names shorter than four characters (E1B, L5I, B1I …) were being parsed wrongly and dropped silently. Fixed, with a test over all of them.
